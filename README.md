# Open Shutter

Open Shutter is an iPhone camera app with the manual controls iOS lets an app touch. Night shots and long exposures out to 30 minutes on any iPhone, shutter speed from the fastest the sensor allows on an iPhone 18 Pro, ISO, white balance in Kelvin and tint, manual focus, exposure compensation, every back lens, and on an iPhone 18 Pro the aperture itself.

It opens straight into the camera. Four modes: Night, Photo, Manual and Video. Settings starts as a short list. A switch at the top, Advanced settings, opens the full one, sorted into groups. The camera is the same either way.

The sensor can't hold a single frame open much longer than a second. Anything longer here is built from frames: a 30 second exposure is thirty 1 second frames added together on the GPU, which is what light does on a sensor anyway, so the brightness matches a real 30 second shot. Night mode works the same way with averaging, and it takes any duration you give it from 1 second to 30 minutes instead of Apple's presets.

It's made for an iPhone 11 or later. The iPhone 18 Pro is the one to have: it's the only phone where the app offers manual shutter speeds, in Photo, Manual and Video and for night shots under a second. The app checks which iPhone it's on. Everywhere else the night shot and the long exposures work the same, set as a duration. Settings has a Phone section that says which you have.

What's in it:

- The shutter chip sits left of the lens buttons and the exposure chip right of them, in the same place in every mode. Without an iPhone 18 Pro the shutter chip is there in Night only, as the duration. Manual adds a row of chips above them, from ISO on, fixed in place and sharing the width. Photo has a readout line in that row instead: ISO, aperture, white balance and focus, with the light meter under it. In Photo the shutter chip shows the camera's own speed, and the first value you pick on its dial switches to Manual. A small A at the top right of any chip means the camera is picking that value, the same mark everywhere. Values the camera picks move in camera stops, so they don't flicker.
- Shutter, ISO, aperture and EV on ruler dials, each one auto or locked. A dial opens above the chips, so nothing under your finger moves. Lock one and the others follow it, so shutter priority, ISO priority and aperture priority all work. With an Xcode 27 build on iOS 27, iOS runs those natively; otherwise a meter loop in the app does. On iOS 26 and later, auto exposure can meter a small spot or the whole frame instead of the area around the point you tap.
- Aperture on variable aperture lenses (iPhone 18 Pro, iOS 27), using the stops Apple recommends for the lens.
- Night is one mode for everything slow. The button left of the shutter, lit and showing the current scene's symbol, lists its scenes. First the night shot: 1 second to 30 minutes of frames averaged into one clean picture, planned for a tripod. Settings > Night has Tripod, Handheld or Auto. Handheld keeps the frames short, lines them up and throws out the blurry ones. Then Manual night, a long exposure with Manual's chips for ISO, white balance and focus, that starts out adding light like one long shutter. Then come the scenes that set themselves up, on auto, with Photo's readout line showing what they chose: Water, Light trails, Stars, Northern lights, Fireworks and Light painting. Stars, for one, is 15 seconds with focus at infinity, white balance at 4000 K and ISO held under 3200, and on an iPhone 18 Pro the iris wide open. In Stars and Northern lights the duration dial turns orange past the longest time that keeps stars as points at that zoom, the old 400 rule, and its title says so: sharp stars to 15s. On those scenes a tap on the readout line opens the focus dial. The tripod scenes wait a moment after the tap, so the press doesn't end up in the picture. Picking the night shot or a scene puts ISO, aperture, white balance and focus back on auto; Manual night is where they're set by hand.
- Each scene brings its own way of combining frames. Real adds light like a long shutter. Blur averages frames, which looks like an ND filter at noon. Trails keeps the brightest pixels for car lights and stars. Manual night takes its from the Blend row in Settings. A dark pill with a stack symbol says what the shot will take, as in 12 frames × 1/4s. The ISO is on the readout line or the ISO chip, so the pill leaves it out. On an iPhone 18 Pro a tap on the pill in a Blur or Trails scene picks the shutter of each frame. Bulb runs until you let go, or until the limit set in Settings, which the pill shows. The moon chip left of the lens buttons sets how long the shot runs.
- White balance presets, Kelvin from 2000 to 10000, tint, and a gray card button.
- Manual focus with focus peaking, tap to focus, a slow drag up or down on the picture for exposure, AE/AF lock, autofocus range, and subject tracking on iOS 27.
- Lens buttons for each back camera plus the 2× crops of the 48 MP sensors (0.5×, 1×, 2×, 4× or 5×, and 8× on a 48 MP telephoto), and a flip button for the front camera. A tap zooms to the lens instead of cutting to it. Touch and hold the buttons and slide, and they turn into a zoom wheel that moves in tenths (1.4×, 2.7×) up to 10× and in whole steps after that. It stops where you let go. Pinch zoom switches lenses too. A lens change holds the last frame and fades into the new camera instead of going dark.
- In Photo and Manual a macro button left of the shutter puts the ultra wide camera at the 1× framing, which focuses far closer. The 1× button stays lit while it's on. Any lens button leaves it, and so does a switch to Night or Video. The button on the right opens the quick settings: timer, format, RAW, resolution, processing, setups, silent shutter, battery saver, histogram, zebras, peaking and false color. If the 4× or 8× lens is too close to focus, the camera moves to a crop of the main camera on its own, the way Apple's does.
- The screen stays in portrait and the buttons stay put however you hold the phone. Photos still come out the right way up.
- Aspect ratios 4:3, 1:1, 16:9, 3:2, 5:4, 2:1, 2.39:1 and 65:24, or any ratio you type in. 16:9 is the default. It fills the width of the screen and the controls sit over the picture, the way Apple's Camera does it. The bands above and below start black and fade to see-through, so at 1:1 you can see what's just outside the frame. Taking a photo blinks the picture black, and a copy of the shot glides into the thumbnail; the buttons stay put. While a Night shot builds, each new picture fades in over the last one, and a tap of the haptics says it saved. Switching modes blurs the last frame into the new one, and whatever is active, the mode, the lens or the chip you're dialling, glows faintly in the accent.
- A tripod sign for Night and any manual shutter built from frames, written straight on the picture with no box behind it: Use a tripod, Hold still or Steady before the shot, Keep still while the frames come in, and The phone moved when it gets bumped. A warning shows in orange. Beside the words a line lies flat and green while the phone is still, and flows like a sound wave when it moves, taller as it turns yellow at a light touch, orange for a hand's tremor and red when it moves. The tripod button on Night's top bar shows or hides the sign, and Settings > Viewfinder has the same switch. Handheld, two crosses show how far the phone has turned since the start, and the job is to keep them on top of each other. Night on a tripod waits a second after the press, and any wait holds on up to 3 seconds more while the phone still moves.
- A battery saver for long shots, off until you turn it on. On a shot of 10 seconds or more, the screen goes black 3 seconds in and lights up again for the last 5 seconds, whenever the phone moves, and for 10 seconds after a touch.
- Self timer up to 10 minutes, an intervalometer (up to 9999 shots, up to an hour apart), and exposure, focus or white balance brackets.
- HEIF, HEIF High, JPEG, JPEG High, PNG and TIFF. RAW is the sensor's data with nothing done to it, and ProRAW is Apple's processed DNG; either one goes alone or next to any of the six. 12 or 48 MP, compression quality, and how much Apple processing you want. PNG and TIFF are lossless: the camera takes the picture at full quality and the app writes it out, at 16 bits for Night. They come without a Live Photo, depth or an HDR gain map.
- A histogram (luma or RGB) at the top middle of the picture, fading to nothing on every side, plus zebras, false color, level and grids. Night has no histogram. The level steps aside while a dial, the zoom wheel or the quick settings are open, and during a countdown or a stack. In Manual a shutter too long for one frame wears a STACKED badge, and a tap on it says what that means.
- Video with manual exposure, frame rate, resolution, HEVC, H.264 or ProRes, HDR, Apple Log or Apple Log 2, every stabilization mode the phone offers, wind noise reduction, and the torch.
- The Camera Control button carries zoom, lens, exposure, shutter, night duration and ISO by default. Night scene, aperture, white balance, tint, focus, timer, flash, histogram and mode can be swapped in from the Camera Control row on Settings' first page. A control that does nothing in the current mode stays in the overlay, dimmed. Each volume button takes a job: shutter, zoom in, zoom out, brighter, darker or next lens. A Camera Control click does what volume down does, and any of them stops a running shot. Haptics come in Off, Light, Normal and Strong, with their own switches for selection ticks and zoom ticks.
- The top bar is always the same eight, drawn as plain thin icons with nothing behind them: flash, aspect ratio, timer, format, histogram, AE lock, Live Photo and Settings. A tap on flash, aspect ratio, timer, format or histogram opens its menu with every choice in it. The format menu has the format, RAW and resolution together. Live Photo, AE lock and the steady sign switch on a tap. One that has nothing to do in a mode dims where it is. In Night the steady sign's switch takes the histogram's place. In Video the timer and format make way for the size and frame rate, each its own menu, and while it records only the light and the clock stay. Solid buttons, under accessibility, puts a dark circle behind each. Grid and level are at the top of Settings, next to the aspect ratio.
- Photo details like Apple's: lens, focal length, aperture, shutter, ISO, exposure mode and compensation, metering, white balance, flash, and where it was taken, but only if you turn on location tags, which start off. It also writes down what Apple's camera has no field for: the mode and scene, which values you set by hand, the zoom, the timer, the bracket a photo belongs to, and for stacked shots the frames, the blend, the time per frame and whether the phone was steady. Your name and copyright go in too, if you type them in Settings.
- A gallery of what you took with the app (it leaves the rest of your library alone), newest at the bottom like Photos. Select picks several to share or delete. Touch and hold a photo to preview it, share it, favourite it or delete it. A Live Photo plays a short hint when you swipe to it. Swipe up on a photo, or tap Info, and a panel slides up under it, laid out like the camera's own controls: the date and time with its time zone, shutter, aperture, ISO and EV with the small A on what the camera picked, the frames and total time of a Night shot, the lens and focal length, white balance, focus, metering, the file type, size and resolution, a histogram that flags clipping, and a map with the place, altitude and which way you faced. There's also a first round of edits: exposure, highlights, shadows, contrast, brightness, saturation, vibrance, warmth, tint, sharpness, vignette, nine looks, crop, straighten, rotate and flip. Edits go into Photos without losing the original, so Revert to original works here or in the Photos app, and Save as copy puts the edit next to the original instead. A Live Photo takes the color changes only, and an HDR photo saves its edit in standard range for now.
- A photo info page in Settings. Open any image from your library or Files and it lists every piece of metadata the file carries. It can save a copy with the location taken out, or with everything taken out.
- Settings in light, dark or whatever the phone uses, with seven accent colors. For accessibility there are larger labels, solid buttons that don't depend on the picture behind them, reduce motion, a left-handed layout, and spoken status, which reads out the countdown and says Keep the phone still and Done. Bold Text, Increase Contrast and VoiceOver work too.
- Preserve settings like Apple's: the mode, the lens and zoom, and the exposure can each stay where you left them or start fresh. Also Prioritize faster shooting, and Allow audio playback, which lets music keep playing while you film.
- Touch and hold lists the choices on the video light's brightness and the manual chips with their common values. The quick settings items with more than two choices open a menu on a tap, like the top bar. Every dial has a Done button that closes it, and a swipe down does the same.
- A focus magnifier, like a camera's live view zoom. Turn the focus dial and the middle of the picture enlarges 5×, or 10× if you set it in Settings, Focus. A tap on the picture, closing the dial or a press of the shutter brings the whole frame back, and that press takes no photo, so nothing is shot cropped. The focus dial has Magnify and Far end buttons.
- Three custom setups, C1 to C3, like a camera's: the mode, lens, exposure, white balance, focus, format, overlays and the Night and video settings of a shot you set up often. They sit after the camera options in Settings: a tap brings one back, and the More button saves the current setup there, renames it or clears it. The quick settings Setups item does the same from the camera.
- Settings opens short: aspect ratio, grid and level at the top, then the few options most people change. With Advanced settings on it has a page each for photos, exposure, focus, the viewfinder, Night, video and controls, and one listing what your camera reports: every format, range and feature.

## Running it

Open `OpenShutter.xcodeproj` and press Run. The Simulator has no camera, so it gets a fake night street with moving car lights instead. That's enough to try every dial, the long exposure trails, night mode and the settings. Xcode 26 and Xcode 27 both build it for the Simulator.

On a phone you need iOS 18 or later. Choose your team under Signing & Capabilities, and turn on Developer Mode on the phone (Settings, Privacy & Security, Developer Mode). With a free Apple ID you also have to trust yourself under Settings, General, VPN & Device Management. The first launch asks for the camera. After that it asks for adding to Photos at the first save, for your library when you open the gallery and tap Continue, for the microphone the first time you switch to video with sound on, and for location only if you turn on location tags.

The iPhone 18 Pro ships with iOS 27, which means Xcode 27; Xcode 26 can't install on it. Everything new in iOS 27 only compiles with Xcode 27 anyway: the aperture, native priority modes, subject tracking and low light video noise reduction. Built with Xcode 26, for the Simulator or a phone on iOS 26, the app treats every lens as fixed aperture and runs priority modes with its own meter loop.

To try the fake camera on a real phone, turn on the `-simulatedCamera` argument in the scheme (Product, Scheme, Edit Scheme, Run, Arguments). Its shots of the fake street go into your real photo library.

The camera math lives in `Packages/CameraCore`: plain Swift and Foundation, with no camera or UI frameworks, so it builds and tests anywhere Swift runs. Cmd+U in Xcode runs its tests, or `swift test` in that folder.

## What it can't do

Apple keeps some things to its own Camera app. Its Night mode, Photographic Styles, Portrait mode rendering and Action mode aren't available to other apps, so they aren't here. The night mode in this app is my own frame stack. It plans for a tripod. Handheld is a setting, decent at best, and it won't beat Apple's on a shaky hand.

Manual exposure only works on the physical lenses, not on Apple's combined multi-lens camera, so a lens change takes a beat and macro is a button rather than automatic.

There's no 24 MP option. iOS only delivers 24 MP through deferred processing, and the app keeps that off so it can crop and save each photo itself. Bayer RAW needs a lens at its own zoom, like 0.5×, 1× or 4×. At 2×, 8×, in macro or with any pinch zoom you get the HEIF or JPEG alone, and RAW in the quick settings says so. ProRAW works at any zoom.

At 16:9 the bands above and below the picture stay black. Apple's Camera fills them from the ultra wide while you shoot on the main lens. This app runs one camera at a time, so there's nothing to put there. If you had an earlier build installed, your saved 4:3 stays until you tap the aspect ratio or reset the settings.

The extra photo details go into each file's XMP, under the namespace `http://ns.katsuma.ca/openshutter/1.0/`, and into videos as QuickTime keys starting with `ca.katsuma.openshutter.`. The Photos app shows the standard fields and ignores the rest; the photo info page shows all of it. Files are named `OpenShutter_` plus the date and time, so they're easy to pick out on a computer.

The battery saver covers the screen in black and turns the brightness down. It can't turn the screen off, because iOS stops the camera when it does.

The sky scenes park the focus at the far end of the lens. Apple doesn't promise that is infinity on every lens, so if stars come out soft, tap the readout line, or the focus chip when the readout is off, to open the focus dial, check them with Magnify and set the focus by hand.

Silent shutter only works where iOS allows it. Elsewhere its quick settings item is dimmed and Settings leaves the row out. Exposure brackets are one burst, capped at what the phone reports, usually three to five frames. Focus brackets (up to 30 frames) and white balance brackets (up to 9) are separate shots. Stacked exposures are built from the video stream, so they're 12 MP even on a 48 MP sensor, and they save as HEIF, JPEG, PNG or TIFF, not RAW.

PNG and TIFF keep the standard photo details. If iOS won't add the app's own XMP to one, the file is saved without it. If Photos won't take a RAW file and its PNG or TIFF as one photo, they go in as two.

If you leave the app in the middle of a long exposure, it keeps the frames it has and finishes the photo when you come back. Apps in the background get no GPU, so that last step has to wait.

The name is Open Shutter because that is what a long exposure is.
